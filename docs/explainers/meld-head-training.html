MELD Head Training
ML route · MELD emotion prototype

Train a small head, keep the big models frozen

A pretrained model already knows general patterns in words and in voices. It does not know MELD's seven emotions. You freeze it, which means its parameters never change. Then you train a small new network on top of it, called the head.

Frozen: parameters never change Trained: parameters change with MELD
The data

What MELD gives you

MELD has 13,708 lines of dialogue from Friends, split into 9,989 training lines, 1,109 dev lines and 2,610 test lines. Each line has the words, a short video clip with its sound, and one emotion label. The labels are very uneven. Neutral lines outnumber fear lines by about 18 to 1.

The process

How training works

Training happens in two stages. The first stage runs the frozen encoders over every line once and saves what they produce. The second stage trains only the head on those saved numbers, many times over. The numbered chips link to the matching code below.

        Choosing a track

        Text and audio, text and vision, or all three

        The brief asks you to pick one main track, either text and audio or text and vision. Using all three inputs is an optional extension for after the core task works. In every option the words go through a text encoder, and the second input is what changes. The rest of this page follows the text and audio track.

        What each extra input adds on MELD

        Papers report the weighted F1 score times 100, so 66.57 means 0.6657. Each bar shows how many points a setup gains over the same paper's text-only model. The words do most of the work. Audio or vision adds between a tenth of a point and about 3 points, and the two are close in most papers. The gains are bigger when the paper's text model is weaker.

        The vision track is hard to prepare on MELD and easy to run live. The audio track is the reverse: simple to prepare, and it needs only the microphone. If people speak to the vision track, it needs the microphone and Whisper anyway, so adding a voice encoder for all three costs about 95M more parameters.

        Gemma 4 and other all-in-one models

        Google released Gemma 4 on 2 April 2026. Its smallest size, E2B, takes text, images and audio in one model and writes text. The name says 2B because Google leaves out the per-layer lookup tables, which hold about 2.3 billion parameters. The challenge counts every parameter, so E2B counts as 5.12 billion. That still fits, with about 0.88 billion to spare. E4B holds 8.0 billion and does not fit.

        On the A40, E2B runs at full 16-bit precision. Google lists 11.4 GB at 16-bit, 5.7 GB at 8-bit and 2.9 GB at 4-bit. llama.cpp can run it with audio. Nobody has published emotion results for Gemma 4, and no Gemma 4 model trained on MELD was found.

        Untrained all-in-one models score low on MELD. Phi-4-multimodal scored 43.4 with no MELD training, against 66 to 67 for the small encoders plus a trained head in the papers above. An all-in-one model also writes its emotion as words, which then have to be matched to one of the seven labels. A trained head gives a score for each label directly. Gemma 4 E2B therefore fits better as the model that writes the reply and turns speech into words, with a trained head still picking the emotion.

        Our results

        Measured on an A40

        Our choices so far

        • Track: text plus vision, chosen on 2026-10-15. Voice encoders now matter only for an optional all-three extension.
        • Speech to text: Whisper large-v3-turbo in int8. Nemotron Streaming was about three times faster, but Whisper keeps the punctuation that carries emotion. On 30 test clips Whisper kept 11 of MELD's 23 exclamation marks, and Nemotron kept none.
        • Text encoder: EmbeddingGemma 300M by default. EmoBERTa-large is kept only as a backup demo, because its weights have likely seen MELD's test lines, so its scores may not be fair.
        • Reply writer: Liquid AI's LFM2.5 1.2B Instruct, the fastest in the reply table below at 122 ms per reply.
        • Vision encoder: not chosen yet. A second A40 run trains heads on frozen vision features, alone and joined with the words.

        On 2026-10-15 we ran the parts of this page on one Runpod A40 with 48 GB. Every score is on MELD's test split of 2,610 lines. Our heads read one line at a time.         Reply writers

        Hugging Face Transformers generate in bf16 at batch size 1, greedy, up to 40 new tokens, over 50 MELD test lines with the gold emotion in the prompt. Reasoning models ran with thinking turned off.

        Nemotron 3 Nano, the Qwen3.5 family and LFM2.5 use Mamba, gated delta-rule or short-convolution layers. They ran on Transformers' slow reference kernels, because causal-conv1d, mamba-ssm and flash-linear-attention were not installed, so their speeds here understate what they reach with those kernels or with vLLM.

        Parameters loaded counts only the part that writes text. For Qwen3.5-4B (4.66B in the library), Qwen3.5-2B (2.27B) and Gemma 4 E2B it leaves out the vision and audio parts that the 6B limit counts.

        Vision-language models with no MELD training

        Each model saw 8 frames from the clip, once alone and once with the spoken line, and picked one of MELD's seven labels. This tests the all-in-one route described above.

        The prediction is the label whose first token the model scores highest as the start of its answer, from one forward pass, so every answer is a label. A first try that let models write freely was thrown away, because Qwen3.5-4B opened 1,579 of 1,585 answers with an explanation instead of a label.

        Each model had a 40-minute limit, so a row may cover a random subset of the 2,457 clips whose frames extracted. Some labels' first tokens are short fragments that can also start other words, so those labels may be picked slightly too often. OneEmo's paper feeds 16 frames with its own prompt and reports a MELD hit rate, which is not weighted F1, so its lower score here is not a direct contradiction of that paper.

        The pod ran from 08:58 to 16:21 UTC, 7.4 hours, for about $4.35 of GPU time at $0.59 an hour. Frames failed to extract for 153 of the 2,610 test clips. The source tables are in ML_route/bench/results/README.md.

        The procedure

        The ten steps, with code

        The step headings are the ten steps of the procedure. The examples use PyTorch and the Hugging Face Transformers library, with RoBERTa-base for the words and WavLM Base+ for the voice.

        1
        Download the MELD dataset.

        The encoders need 16 kHz mono sound, so this step also pulls the sound out of each video clip with ffmpeg.

        # The labels: one CSV row per line of dialogue
wget https://raw.githubusercontent.com/declare-lab/MELD/master/data/MELD/train_sent_emo.csv
wget https://raw.githubusercontent.com/declare-lab/MELD/master/data/MELD/test_sent_emo.csv

# The clips (about 10.9 GB): train, dev and test archives of .mp4 files named dia6_utt1.mp4 and so on
wget https://huggingface.co/datasets/declare-lab/MELD/resolve/main/MELD.Raw.tar.gz
tar -xzf MELD.Raw.tar.gz
# Unpack the inner train archive too, then point CLIPS at its folder of .mp4 files
CLIPS=MELD.Raw/train_splits

mkdir -p wav/train
for f in "$CLIPS"/*.mp4; do
  ffmpeg -loglevel error -i "$f" -ac 1 -ar 16000 "wav/train/$(basename "${f%.mp4}").wav"
done
        2–3
        Load the pretrained model. Freeze all the parameters in the pretrained model.

        Setting requires_grad = False tells PyTorch never to compute an update for those parameters.

        import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModel, AutoFeatureExtractor

# Step 2: load the two pretrained encoders
text_tok  = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("FacebookAI/roberta-base")
text_enc  = AutoModel.from_pretrained("FacebookAI/roberta-base").eval()
audio_fx  = AutoFeatureExtractor.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus")
audio_enc = AutoModel.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus").eval()

# Step 3: freeze them, so training can never change their parameters
for p in list(text_enc.parameters()) + list(audio_enc.parameters()):
    p.requires_grad = False
        4–5
        Send each MELD line through the pretrained model. Record the output numbers for each line.

        The encoders never change, so their output for a line never changes either. You compute it once and save it. Run the same loop on test_sent_emo.csv to make test_features.pt.

        import os
import pandas as pd
import soundfile as sf

LABELS = ["anger", "disgust", "fear", "joy", "neutral", "sadness", "surprise"]

@torch.no_grad()
def features(text, audio):
    t = text_tok(text, return_tensors="pt", truncation=True)
    t_vec = text_enc(**t).last_hidden_state[:, 0]          # 768 numbers for the words
    a = audio_fx(audio, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt")
    a_vec = audio_enc(**a).last_hidden_state.mean(dim=1)   # 768 numbers for the voice
    return torch.cat([t_vec, a_vec], dim=1)                # 1,536 numbers per line

# Step 4: send every training line through the frozen encoders
rows = pd.read_csv("train_sent_emo.csv")
X, y = [], []
for r in rows.itertuples():
    path = f"wav/train/dia{r.Dialogue_ID}_utt{r.Utterance_ID}.wav"
    if not os.path.exists(path):       # a few clips are missing or broken
        continue
    audio, _ = sf.read(path, dtype="float32")
    X.append(features(r.Utterance, audio))
    y.append(LABELS.index(r.Emotion))

# Step 5: record the features, so this slow part runs only once
torch.save({"X": torch.cat(X), "y": torch.tensor(y)}, "train_features.pt")
        6
        Connect a head that has seven outputs.

        This head takes the 1,536 numbers, mixes them through 256 hidden numbers, and gives one score per emotion. It is the only part with parameters that training will change.

        import torch.nn as nn

head = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(1536, 256),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Dropout(0.2),
    nn.Linear(256, 7),     # one score per emotion
)
print(sum(p.numel() for p in head.parameters()))   # 395271
        7
        Train the head with the features from the training lines.

        Each pass shows the head every training line once. The loss measures how wrong each guess is. The optimizer then adjusts the head's parameters, and only those, to make the loss smaller. Because MELD is so uneven, the loss counts a mistake on a rare emotion more heavily. A real run would also check the dev set after each pass and keep the best head.

        data = torch.load("train_features.pt")
X, y = data["X"], data["y"]

# Rare emotions weigh more: fear has 268 training lines, neutral has 4,710
counts = torch.bincount(y, minlength=7).float()
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss(weight=counts.sum() / (7 * counts))
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(head.parameters(), lr=1e-3)   # the head's parameters only

head.train()
for epoch in range(20):
    order = torch.randperm(len(y))
    for i in range(0, len(y), 64):
        batch = order[i:i + 64]
        loss = loss_fn(head(X[batch]), y[batch])
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    print(epoch, round(loss.item(), 3))
        8–9
        Measure the head with the test lines. Record the weighted F1 score.

        The weighted F1 score is a number from 0 to 1 that shows how correct the labels are. Each emotion counts in proportion to how often it appears in the test lines. MELD papers report this score, so you can compare with them.

        from sklearn.metrics import f1_score, classification_report

test = torch.load("test_features.pt")
head.eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    guess = head(test["X"]).argmax(dim=1)

# Step 8: measure the head on 2,610 lines it never saw
print(classification_report(test["y"].numpy(), guess.numpy(), target_names=LABELS))

# Step 9: record the weighted F1 score
print("weighted F1:", f1_score(test["y"].numpy(), guess.numpy(), average="weighted"))
        10
        Save the head.

        The saved file is about 1.6 MB. The encoders are not saved, because they are still exactly as you downloaded them.

        torch.save(head.state_dict(), "head.pt")
        After training

        Live use

        In the demo, everything runs on one Runpod pod. A MELD scene is played clip by clip at real-time speed, and each clip holds one spoken line. When a clip ends, Whisper turns its sound into words. The frozen encoders and the trained head turn the words and the clip into an emotion. A small language model then writes a reply from the words and the emotion. One line therefore produces both outputs the challenge asks for.

        from faster_whisper import WhisperModel
from transformers import pipeline

asr  = WhisperModel("large-v3-turbo", device="cuda", compute_type="int8")
chat = pipeline("text-generation", model="LiquidAI/LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct", device="cuda")
head.load_state_dict(torch.load("head.pt"))
head.eval()

def on_line(audio):
    """Runs once for each clip, when it ends (16 kHz mono)."""
    segments, _ = asr.transcribe(audio, language="en")
    text = " ".join(s.text for s in segments).strip()

    with torch.no_grad():
        probs = head(features(text, audio)).softmax(dim=1)[0]
    state = {"emotion": LABELS[probs.argmax().item()],
             "confidence": round(probs.max().item(), 2)}            # output 1

    prompt = (f'Someone said "{text}" and sounds {state["emotion"]}. '
              "Reply in one short, kind sentence.")
    out = chat([{"role": "user", "content": prompt}], max_new_tokens=40)
    reply = out[0]["generated_text"][-1]["content"].strip()                               # output 2
    return state, reply

        The loop that plays a scene's clips in order and calls on_line as each one ends is left out. The MELD player in ML_route/player/ does this today, with random emotions in place of the head.
